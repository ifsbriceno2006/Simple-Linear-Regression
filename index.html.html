    Meet the shop and its data

    You are the assistant manager of a home and office supplies shop in Belize City. Each week you write down three numbers. Your manager asks one question: which of these helps us predict sales better, advertising or price?

    🛒 Shop-floor view

    Think of the till at the shop. Each week you note how much you spent on advertising and how much the till rang up. After a few weeks you ask: if I know the advertising spend, can I estimate the week’s takings? A method for building that estimate from the shop’s own records is called regression. The takings you want to estimate are Y. The clue you use is X.

    Y: the response

    What we want to explain or predict. Here: weekly sales revenue in BZD. It goes on the vertical axis.

    X: the predictor

    A clue we use to predict Y. Here we have two candidates: advertising spending and average selling price.

    One dot per week

    A scatterplot puts one dot at (X, Y) for each week. Dots drifting up ↗ mean a positive link, down ↘ a negative link, a shapeless cloud means a weak link.

    Careful with the word “independent”: calling X the independent variable does not mean X is unrelated to sales. It only means we are using X as the clue.

    The line of best fit, and the misses

    Regression draws one straight line through the dots. But which line? Let’s find out by trying to beat the computer.

    🛒 Shop-floor view

    Imagine pinning the weekly records on the back-office wall and stretching a piece of string across the dots, so it runs as close to all of them as possible. For each week, measure the straight up-or-down gap between the takings the till actually showed and where the string says they should have been. That gap is the miss. Statisticians call it a residual.

    The line is written Ŷ = a + b·X where Ŷ (“Y-hat”) is the predicted sales, a is the intercept (where the line starts) and b is the slope (how steep it is).

    Residual = actual − predicted = Y − Ŷ. A positive residual means the dot is above the line (sales beat the prediction). A negative residual means the dot is below it.

    Why squares? Ups and downs would cancel if we just added the misses. Squaring makes every miss positive, and it makes big misses count extra. Least squares picks the a and b that make the sum of the squared misses as small as possible. That is the “best” line.

    Slope and intercept: what the numbers mean

    The line has two numbers. Each one tells a small story in BZD.

    🛒 Shop-floor view

    Slope answers: “for each extra BZD 1 spent on advertising, how many extra BZD land in the till?” Intercept is the starting point: the takings the line predicts for a week with no advertising at all.

    Predicted sales = 2305.63 + 5.5161 × Advertising

    Slope b = 5.5161

    BZD of sales revenue per BZD of advertising. So BZD 100 more advertising goes with about BZD 551.61 more weekly sales revenue. It is revenue, not profit.

    Intercept a = 2305.63

    Predicted sales when advertising is 0. But the lowest advertising we ever saw was BZD 300, so this is an extrapolation and not a reliable “no-ads baseline”.

    🎮 Try it: walk along the line

    ✅ Interpolation vs ⚠️ extrapolation

    Interpolation means predicting inside the range of X we actually observed (BZD 300 to 1,000). That is defensible. Extrapolation means predicting outside it. The line keeps going on the screen, but we have no evidence the shop behaves that way at BZD 1,500. A point prediction is never a guarantee.

    Correlation r: the togetherness meter

    How tightly do the dots hug a straight line, and which way does it lean? One number, r, answers both.

    🌱 Start here: the simplest version

    Some things in the shop go up and down together. When it is very hot, more cold drinks sell. When it rains, fewer people walk in.

    r is a number that tells you how much two things go up and down together.

    • r near +1: when one goes up, the other goes up too. Almost every time.
    • r near −1: when one goes up, the other goes down. Almost every time.
    • r near 0: no pattern. Knowing one tells you nothing about the other.

    In our shop, weeks with more advertising had more sales. That is why r is close to +1 (it is 0.98).

    🛒 Shop-floor view

    Think of r as a togetherness meter from −1 to +1. At +1 the dots march in a perfect line uphill. At −1 they march perfectly downhill. At 0 they look like loose change spilled across the counter. The sign tells you the direction. The size (ignore the sign) tells you the strength.

    🎮 Try it: slide r and watch the dots

    🎮 Try it: build your own dots

    Click inside the chart to add dots and watch r change. Then try the buttons.

    What r is really measuring: do the weeks “agree”?

    Every week is either above or below average for X, and above or below average for Y. r checks whether weeks usually land on the same side for both. Weeks that agree (both above, or both below) push r up. Weeks that disagree pull r down. Weeks very far from average count for more than weeks near it.

    🎮 Try it: agree or disagree?

    Teal areas = weeks that agree. Coral areas = weeks that disagree. Tap a dot to check a week.

    Back to the shop

    Advertising: r = +0.9834

    Strong, upward. Dots hug the line.

    Average price: r = −0.2222

    Weak, downward. Dots are scattered. Compare |r| values (0.9834 vs 0.2222), not raw slopes, because slopes are in different units.

    ⚠️ Watch out

    r only measures straight-line togetherness. A clear curve can still give r near 0. And even r = 0.98 does not prove one thing causes the other. A scatterplot first, a number second.

    ✅ What r tells you

    The direction (+ or −) and tightness of a straight-line pattern between two columns of your data.

    ❌ What r does not tell you

    It is not the slope. It is not a percentage. r = 0.5 is not “half as good” as 1. And it never proves cause.

    🗣️ How to say it in a report

    “There is a strong positive linear association between weekly advertising and weekly sales (r = +0.98).”

    🔎 How to use it in analysis

    Look at the plot first. Then use r to rank clues: advertising (|r| = 0.98) is a far better predictor of sales than price (|r| = 0.22).

    📋 In your spreadsheet

    =CORREL(Data!D2:D21,Data!B2:B21) gives signed r. Excel’s regression output shows “Multiple R”, which is always |r| and hides the sign, so keep CORREL for the direction.

    R²: how much of the wiggle does the line explain?

    Sales go up and down from week to week. R² tells you what share of that up-and-down the line accounts for.

    🌱 Start here: the simplest version

    Sales go up and down from week to week. Some of that moving around happens because we spent more or less on advertising. The rest happens for other reasons: holidays, weather, luck.

    R² is a share. It tells you how much of the up-and-down comes from advertising.

    R² = 0.97 means: out of every 100 bits of up-and-down, about 97 come from advertising and 3 come from other things. R² = 0.05 means advertising explains only about 5 out of 100.

    🛒 Shop-floor view

    Two shop managers each estimate the weekly takings. Manager A always predicts the usual average week. Manager B uses the advertising line. Add up each manager’s squared misses over the 20 weeks. If B’s total is tiny compared with A’s, the line is doing a lot of work. R² is the share of A’s misses that B removes.

    R² = 1 − (squared misses with the line) ÷ (squared misses with the average)

    🎮 Try it: Manager A vs Manager B

    Explained by the lineLeft over (residual)

    In simple regression (one X, with an intercept), R² = r². So r = 0.9834 gives R² = 0.9671, and price’s r = −0.2222 gives R² = 0.0494. Squaring also removes the sign, so R² says nothing about direction.

    🎮 Try it: from r to R²

    🎮 Try it: 100 squares

    Each square is 1 out of every 100 bits of up-and-down in weekly sales. Teal squares are explained by the line. Pink squares are left to other things.

    🗣️ How to say it in a report

    “Advertising accounts for about 96.7% of the week-to-week variation in sales in these 20 weeks (R² = 0.9671).”

    📏 Is my R² “good”?

    It depends on the field. Predicting machine output in a lab, you might expect 0.99. For people’s buying behaviour, 0.30 can already be useful. Compare with other clues, not with a magic number.

    ⚠️ A high R² can still mislead

    R² can be high while residuals show a curve, while one odd week drives everything, or while the link is not causal. Always pair R² with the plots.

    ⚠️ Watch out

    R² = 0.9671 does not mean predictions are 96.7% accurate, and it does not mean advertising “causes 96.7% of sales”. It means the line accounts for 96.7% of the sample’s up-and-down in sales around the average. The leftover share is 1 − R² = 3.3%.

    Adjusted R²: the “don’t just add stuff” penalty

    🛒 Shop-floor view

    Imagine scoring a shop’s product range so that every extra product line earns a point, even one that never sells. That would be unfair. Adjusted R² takes a point away for each extra line, so only the lines that earn their shelf space come out ahead.

    Formula: adjusted R² = 1 − (1 − R²)(n − 1)/(n − k − 1), where k is the number of predictors. Here n = 20 and k = 1, giving 0.9652, slightly below R². Plain R² can never go down when you add a predictor. Adjusted R² can.

    🎮 Try it: add useless predictors

    Pretend extra predictors add nothing, so R² stays at 0.9671. What happens to adjusted R²?

    📋 In your spreadsheet

    =RSQ(Model!B2:B21,Model!A2:A21) for R², and =1-(1-B5)*(B2-1)/(B2-2) for adjusted R². “Adjusted R Square” is adjusted R², not an adjusted r, so do not take its square root.

    The typical miss, and checking the line is fair

    R² says how much the line explains. Two more questions matter: how big is a typical miss in BZD? and is a straight line even the right shape?

    🛒 Shop-floor view

    Each week you estimate the takings from the advertising spend. The residual standard error is how far off that estimate typically is: the usual gap between your estimate and what the till actually shows. It is measured in BZD of sales. It is not the biggest miss, and it is not a percentage.

    s = √( squared misses ÷ (n − 2) ) = BZD 237.68

    Why n − 2 = 18? We used the data to work out two numbers (a and b) before measuring the misses, which uses up two “free pieces” of information. These leftover pieces are the residual degrees of freedom.

    🎮 Try it: how many dots fall inside the bands?

    Is the line the right shape? Look at the residuals

    Plot the residuals against the predicted values. If a straight line is a fair summary, you should see a random cloud around zero: no curve, no funnel, no lonely points.

    L: Linear

    The average of Y really moves in a straight line with X. A curve in the residuals breaks this.

    I: Independent

    One week’s miss doesn’t predict the next week’s. Check residuals in week order.

    N: Normal errors

    Misses are roughly bell-shaped. Matters most for exact small-sample tests and intervals.

    E: Equal spread

    The size of the misses stays about the same everywhere. A funnel breaks this.

    🎮 Try it: residual plots, healthy and unhealthy

    🕹️ Game: spot the pattern

    A mystery residual plot appears. What does it show?

    Pick an answer.
    ⚠️ Watch out

    With only 20 weeks, plots can’t prove the rules hold. They can only warn you when something looks wrong. Investigate unusual weeks (maybe a typo, maybe a holiday). Don’t delete them just to raise R².

    📋 In your spreadsheet

    Residual standard error: =INDEX(LINEST(Model!B2:B21,Model!A2:A21,TRUE,TRUE),3,2). Residual plot: chart Model!C1:D21 (predicted on X, residual on Y). Week plot: Week on X, residual on Y.

    The p-value: is the pattern real, or just luck?

    Our slope is 5.52. Maybe advertising really matters, or maybe the dots just happened to line up. How could we tell?

    🌱 Start here: the simplest version

    Here is the one question the p-value answers: “If nothing special was going on, would I be surprised to see this?”

    A friend says she can tell heads from tails with her eyes closed. She tries 10 flips and gets all 10 right. If she was only guessing, that would almost never happen. You would be very surprised. So maybe she is not just guessing.

    If she got 6 right out of 10, that happens all the time by luck. You would not be surprised. So you cannot say she has a special skill.

    • Small p (like 0.001): very surprising if nothing is going on. So we believe something is going on.
    • Big p (like 0.35): not surprising at all. Luck could easily explain it.

    For the shop, “nothing is going on” means advertising has no effect on sales. Our result is far too surprising for that idea, so we don’t believe it.

    🎮 Try it: the guessing game

    Pretend 1,000 people each just guess 10 coin flips. The bars show how many of them got each score. Pick your friend’s score to see how surprising it would be.

    🔗 Now connect it to regression

    The pretend guessers are “nothing is going on”. In the shop version, nothing going on means advertising does not affect sales. Our real slope plays the part of your friend’s score. The shuffle test below makes pretend weeks to see how often luck alone gives a slope as steep as ours. That share of luck-only results is the p-value.

    🛒 Shop-floor view

    A skeptical business partner says: “Advertising and takings have nothing to do with each other. Your dots lined up by luck.” To test that, write each week’s till total on a slip of paper, shuffle the slips, and pin them to the weeks at random. Now advertising and takings truly have nothing to do with each other. Work out the slope. Do it again and again. If your real slope is far steeper than anything shuffling can make, the skeptic is in trouble. The p-value is how often shuffling alone gives a slope as extreme as yours.

    🎮 Try it: the shuffle test

    Latest shuffle (sales dealt out at random)
    Slopes from many shuffles

    The textbook version: hypotheses, t and p

    Statisticians write the skeptic’s claim as H₀: β₁ = 0 (no linear relationship in the population) against H₁: β₁ ≠ 0. Before looking, pick a cut-off called α = 0.05.

    t = slope ÷ SE(slope) = 5.5161 ÷ 0.2399 = 22.99

    t counts “how many standard errors away from zero is my slope?”. The p-value is the chance, if H₀ were true and the model assumptions hold, of getting a t at least this far from 0. Smaller p means the skeptic’s world makes our result look weirder.

    • p < 0.05: reject H₀. There is evidence of a linear association.
    • p ≥ 0.05: fail to reject H₀. That is not the same as proving there is no relationship. We just don’t have enough evidence.

    🎮 Try it: t and the tails

    🧮 Calculator: your own t and p

    🎮 Try it: the surprise meter

    Pick a p-value and see what it says in plain words.

    Not surprisingExtremely surprising

    🎮 Try it: what makes p small?

    Three things shrink p: a stronger link, more weeks of data, and less scatter. Try changing the strength and the number of weeks.

    The same strength of link gives a smaller p with more weeks. So a tiny p can come from a large sample even when the link is weak. That is why you read r, R² and the slope too.

    Three myths (tap to reveal)

    ❌ “p = 0.03 means there’s a 3% chance the null hypothesis is true.”

    No. The p-value assumes H₀ is true and asks how surprising the data are. It is not the probability that H₀ is true, and not the probability the result is “just chance”.

    ❌ “A tiny p-value means advertising has a big or causal effect.”

    No. Tiny p says the pattern is hard to blame on luck alone. Size comes from the slope (BZD 551.61 per BZD 100). Cause needs more than a p-value: holidays, promotions or item mix might drive both advertising and sales.

    ❌ “p = 0.35 proves price has no effect on sales.”

    No. It means these 20 weeks don’t give enough evidence of a straight-line link. Try the price test above: shuffling alone often beats the real slope.

    ✅ Same test, two names

    With one predictor, “Significance F” in the ANOVA table and the slope’s p-value answer the same question. Also F = t². Very tiny p-values can differ in the last digits between software packages. Report p < 0.001.

    Putting r, R² and p together

    Each number answers a different question. Together they tell the whole story.

    ✅ A routine you can reuse on any data set

    1. Look at the plot. Is there a straight-line pattern? Any odd weeks?
    2. How tight? Read r: direction and strength.
    3. How much does it explain? Read R².
    4. Could it be luck? Read the p-value.
    5. How big, and how sure? Read the slope and its confidence interval in real units (BZD).
    6. Does it make business sense? Check capacity, costs and other explanations before acting.

    📋 In your spreadsheet

    =T.DIST.2T(ABS(B13),B12) turns the t statistic and residual df into a two-sided p-value. Format very small p-values in scientific notation.

    Confidence intervals and margin of error

    Our slope, 5.52, came from just 20 weeks. A different 20 weeks would give a slightly different slope. How much wiggle room should we allow?

    🌱 Start here: the simplest version

    The shop wrote down 20 weeks. The slope came out as 5.52. But if we had written down a different 20 weeks, we would get a slightly different number, maybe 5.3 or 5.8.

    So 5.52 is a good guess, not a perfect fact. A confidence interval is the “give or take” around the guess. The margin of error is the give-or-take part.

    We say: 5.52 give or take 0.50. So the real slope is probably between 5.01 and 6.02.

    • Narrow interval: a precise guess. We know the slope well.
    • Wide interval: an unsure guess. We need more data.

    More weeks of data and tighter dots make the interval narrower. Asking to be more sure makes it wider.

    🎮 Try it: different weeks, different slope

    These are pretend weeks made from a shop whose true slope is exactly 5.516. Each time you take 20 new weeks, the fitted line wobbles a little. The size of that wobble is the standard error of the slope.

    Latest 20 pretend weeks
    Slopes from all the samples so far
    🛒 Shop-floor view

    A supplier tells you “delivery in about 2 to 3 days”. Not exactly 2.4, but a best estimate with room on both sides. That room is the margin of error. For our slope: best guess ± margin of error = confidence interval.

    CI = b ± t* × SE(b) = 5.5161 ± 2.1009 × 0.2399 = [5.012, 6.020]
    • SE(b) = how much the slope typically wobbles from sample to sample.
    • t* = a multiplier from the t distribution (2.1009 for 95% with 18 df). Higher confidence means a bigger multiplier.
    • Margin of error = t* × SE = 0.504.

    🎮 Try it: shrink or stretch the margin

    For the n slider we imagine more weeks that look like ours (same spread of advertising and same typical miss).

    What does “95% confident” really mean?

    Imagine 20 parallel universes where the shop’s true slope is exactly 5.516, and in each one you record 20 weeks and build an interval. Most intervals catch the truth. A few miss. 95% describes the long-run hit rate of the method, not the chance for one finished interval.

    🎮 Try it: parallel universes

    Teal = caught the true slope. Coral = missed. Uses the level you picked above.

    🎮 Try it: does the interval include zero?

    Zero means “no effect”. If the interval for the slope includes 0, we cannot rule out that there is no effect. That matches what a large p-value says.

    Margin of error for a forecast: two different questions

    Interval for the mean

    “What is the average sales across many weeks with this advertising?” Narrow.

    Prediction interval

    “What might one particular week’s sales be?” Wider, because it adds the week-to-week wiggle.

    🎮 Try it: forecast bands

    ⚠️ Watch out

    Notice the bands flare out far from the middle of the data. And these intervals only work if the model assumptions hold. They also ignore the fact that we picked advertising after looking at the data. Confirm important decisions with new weeks.

    🗣️ Say it in a report

    “We are 95% confident the true slope is between 5.01 and 6.02 BZD of weekly sales per BZD of advertising.”

    💰 Translate it for the manager

    “Each extra BZD 100 of weekly advertising is associated with about BZD 501 to BZD 602 more weekly sales.”

    ❌ Don’t say

    “95% of weeks fall in this range”, or “there is a 95% chance the true slope is in this interval”.

    📏 Width tells you precision

    A narrow interval means a precise estimate. A very wide one means: collect more weeks before deciding.

    📋 In your spreadsheet

    =T.INV.2T(0.05,B12) gives t*. Then lower = =B9-B17*B10 and upper = =B9+B17*B10. For forecasts use h = 1/n + (X₀ − mean X)²/DEVSQ(X); the mean interval uses √h and the prediction interval uses √(1+h).

    Sneak peek: multiple regression

    So far one clue (X) predicted sales. But the shop recorded two clues. What if we use both at once? That is multiple linear regression, and it is the topic for next week.

    🛒 Shop-floor view

    Sales in any week depend on more than one thing: advertising, the prices on the shelf, what is in stock. Simple regression looks at one factor at a time. Multiple regression looks at several together and asks: if I change just this one factor and keep the others the same, what happens to takings?

    Ŷ = b₀ + b₁·Advertising + b₂·Price

    Each coefficient now has a “holding the others constant” meaning. b₁ is the change in predicted sales for BZD 1 more advertising among weeks with the same average price.

    ⚠️ Just a preview

    In your lab you fit only the simple model and are told not to fit a multiple regression. This chapter is a peek ahead using the same data. Next week we’ll build it properly.

    🎮 Look: what’s left after advertising has done its job?

    Left axis: sales leftovers after advertising (the residuals). Bottom axis: the part of price that advertising doesn’t already explain. If price still mattered, these dots would show a clear slope.

    🎮 Try it: predict with two clues

    Simple vs multiple on the same 20 weeks

    What happened?

    Adding price nudged R² up a hair, but price’s own p-value is large. Price adds little once advertising is in.

    Why did the advertising slope move?

    Because each coefficient now adjusts for the other. Advertising and price are slightly related, so they share a little information.
